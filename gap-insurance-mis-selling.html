GAP insurance mis-selling

Home / Guides / GAP insurance mis-selling

GAP insurance is mis-sold when the way it was sold breaks the rules, or leaves you with a policy that was not described properly. A price you later wish you had not paid is not, on its own, the same thing. The FCA has still been clear that parts of this market were poor value, and in 2024 it paused sales until firms improved that value. If you have a complaint, you go to the firm first, then the Financial Ombudsman Service. The Ombudsman is free.

What a poor sale looks like

The rules a dealer sale has to follow are in ICOBS 6A.1, and they are explained with a calendar example in the FCA rules guide. A sale is in trouble when those rules were skipped:

  • You were told the policy was compulsory, or it was added without a clear choice. The firm has to tell you whether it is optional or compulsory. A separate FCA rule, ICOBS 6A.2, says you must actively choose an optional product. Silence, or a box that was already ticked, is not a choice.
  • The premium was not shown separately from the car or the finance.
  • You were not told that other distributors sell GAP. The FCA's competition remedy exists because so many add-on buyers never compared.
  • The dealer concluded the sale before two clear days had passed, and you did not start an earlier sale yourself under the exception.
  • You were sold finance GAP when you paid cash and had no finance to clear, and nobody explained that.
  • You already had new-car replacement on your motor policy, and the sale was presented as the only way to replace the car. What you were told, and what you needed, is fact-specific. The Ombudsman looks at that evidence.

Poor value is not automatically a personal refund

In February 2024 the FCA said only 6% of GAP premiums were paid out in claims, and that it had seen firms paying 70% of premium value in commission. Firms paused sales. In May 2024 some were allowed to restart with lower commission. Those are market findings about fair value. They do not mean every customer who paid a high price is due their premium back. They do mean price and commission were serious enough for the regulator to stop sales until firms could justify them.

A dealer policy that costs more than an online one, after you were told it was optional, given the information, and given the wait, is not mis-selling just because you later find a cheaper quote. The dealer vs online guide is about that comparison. Use the cancellation right if you are still in time. The refund rules and a mis-selling complaint are different tools.

An example of a complaint, and one that is weaker

Example only

£790 added on collection day, and a £400 policy that was properly offered

On collection day a salesperson says the finance will not go through unless GAP is taken. The £790 premium is folded into the monthly payment. Nothing in durable form says the cover is optional, names a separate premium, or says that other sellers exist. The contract is signed that afternoon. That pattern collides with the prescribed information, the deferral, and the need for an active choice. The customer asks for the £790 back. Whether the complaint is upheld depends on the documents and what was said. This is an illustration of a complaint, not a decision about a real firm.

A different customer is given the information on a Monday, waits until Thursday, and chooses a £400 dealer policy over a £220 online policy because they want it handled in the showroom. They understood it was optional. The fact that a cheaper policy existed does not, by itself, make that sale a mis-sale. The £400 and £220 are example prices, not a survey.

These pound figures are an illustration so you can see the arithmetic. They are not a quote, a market average, or a claim paid by a named insurer.

How to complain

The Financial Ombudsman Service sets the order out:

  1. Complain to the firm that sold the policy, or to the insurer on the schedule. Say what happened and how you want it put right. Keep emails, letters and a note of calls.
  2. For most insurance complaints the firm has up to eight weeks to investigate. It should send a final response that tells you the outcome and how to go further.
  3. If you are unhappy with that response, or the eight weeks pass without one, you can bring the complaint to the Ombudsman. Do this within six months of the date on the final response. You do not need a claims company.

Look the firm up on the Financial Services Register if you are unsure who is responsible. If you are still deciding whether you want the policy at all, and you are inside 14 days, cancelling may be quicker than a complaint. If the problem is that you were never given a fair chance to say no, complain.

Common questions

Can I complain after the policy has ended?
Time limits apply, and the Financial Ombudsman Service publishes them. Complain to the firm as soon as you realise there is a problem. Do not wait for the policy to finish if you already know the sale was wrong.
Will a refund include interest?
If a complaint is upheld, the firm or the Ombudsman can add interest. There is no single rate this site can quote as yours. The decision in your case sets it.
Should I pay a claims company?
The Financial Ombudsman Service says its service is free and that you do not need to pay a lawyer or a claims management company to use it. A friend or family member can help you. A company that takes a cut of a refund is optional, not required.
The dealer has closed. Who do I complain to?
Complain to the insurer named on the policy schedule, and to the finance firm if they introduced or sold the policy. The Financial Services Register lists authorised firms and is the FCA's source for their contact details.
